# Thumbnail guide

The thumbnail has one job: stop the scroll at phone size and promise the payoff. The deliverable is a copy-paste prompt for the creator's image tool, plus a list of images to upload with it. Give two concepts, so they can test one against the other.

Use the creator's channel profile for colours, fonts, faces and text. The rules below fill the gaps and catch the common mistakes.

## The text: add what the title doesn't say

The thumbnail text has exactly one job: add the one piece of information the title doesn't give. The click closes that gap.

**The overlap test.** Write the title. Write the candidate text. Ask: if a viewer reads the title, do they already know this? If yes, the thumbnail carries nothing and you have wasted the most valuable space on the page.

This fails without anyone noticing. For a video titled "How to get your whole team posting on LinkedIn", a first draft of "EVERYONE POSTS" is the title again in two words. The fix is to jump to what happens when everyone posts, using a result the video actually shows: "12X THE REACH".

**Outcome, not mechanism.** The title often describes what happens in the video. The thumbnail shows what the viewer ends up with. Before and after works well. A real number beats an adjective. Take the number from the video so the promise is true.

**Where to find the outcome.** Look in the transcript for: the number the video cites, the state the viewer reaches, or the problem that stops being a problem. Any of those, said in the video, is honest on a thumbnail.

**Text rules**
- 3 words is the sweet spot, 4 or 5 the ceiling. Fewer words get read; long text gets skipped.
- Heavy, blocky sans-serif (Anton, Bebas Neue, Montserrat ExtraBold, Oswald; Poppins for a friendlier feel), unless the profile names a font.
- Big: roughly 100 to 200 px tall on a 1280 x 720 canvas.
- Always a stroke, shadow or colour block behind the text so it reads on any background.

## The picture: point the same way as the wish

Getting the words right isn't enough. Run the same check on the image.

A common miss: a stat card showing a big number with an arrow to a smaller number (say, "all your contacts" filtered down to "real leads"). The story may be good, but the picture is the visual grammar of loss. Nobody wishes for less. Before and after works when it runs small to big, mess to order, empty to full.

Before you hand over a concept, ask:
- **Does the image show more, or less?** A shrinking number, a shorter bar or a narrowing funnel reads as subtraction at a glance.
- **Am I drawing the mechanism or the wish?** Filtering, sorting and "X% qualified" are what the video does. The viewer wants the result. Draw the result.
- **Is the main argument in the smallest text?** Cover the small text. If the concept falls apart, it was never carrying it. Show it instead: rows of job titles like "Founder", "CEO" make "these are buyers" visible without explanation.
- **Can the viewer place themselves in the number?** A big personal number means little to someone with a tenth of it. A number is worth the space when viewers can picture it for themselves, or when it signals sheer volume.

A plain white card with two figures is a slide, not a thumbnail. Slides convince people who are already listening. A thumbnail has to grab someone who wants nothing from you yet. Abundance works: a stack of rows running past the edge of the frame reads as "lots".

## Composition

- One focal point. Remove background clutter, or blur and darken it.
- Two or three visual elements in total. One arrow at most: one arrow looks deliberate, three look messy.
- Keep text and face out of the bottom-right corner (YouTube puts the duration there) and away from all edges (phones crop them).
- A face, if used, fills about a quarter to a third of the frame and doesn't touch the text.
- For "connect X to Y" or tool videos: two logos joined by one connector (arrow, plus sign, glowing line) is instantly readable.
- Hybrid layout: face on one side, logos or a cropped piece of UI on the other. Human trust plus "here's the thing".
- For software tutorials, show the one important part of the screen (the button, the chart, the result), cropped tight. Never a full busy screenshot.

## Faces

- Only from an uploaded photo. Never a generated or "improved" face.
- **Never prescribe the expression.** The prompt says: keep the expression exactly as it is in the uploaded photo. The creator picks the photo, and with it the expression. Don't add "excited" or "surprised", and don't add "keep it subtle" either.
- Eyes look at the camera, or at the thing you want noticed.
- For educational or B2B channels, calm and clear tends to beat dramatic. Viewers looking for help want to trust you. Mention this when the creator picks a photo; never put it in the prompt.

## Colour

- The subject is clearly brighter or darker than the background. Strong contrast is the most common trait of thumbnails that get clicked.
- Two or three colours. Not more.
- Accent colour: not blue, red, white or black, which blend into YouTube's interface. Orange, amber, yellow, teal and lime separate well. A brand blue is fine as the background, with a non-blue accent on top.

## Technical

1280 x 720, 16:9, PNG for the sharpest text, under 2 MB. Keep style and layout consistent across videos so the channel becomes recognisable.

Image tools often return a different size or shape. Ask for the important parts in the centre, and tell the creator to crop to 1280 x 720 afterwards. Image tools also misspell words: keep text short and check every letter.

## Prompt template: concept A (face plus graphic)

Tell the creator to upload their headshot and any logos or screenshots as reference images. Fill the brackets from the profile and the video.

```
A YouTube thumbnail, 1280x720, 16:9, clean and high-contrast, readable at small phone size.

Left third: the person from the uploaded headshot. Keep their face and their expression exactly
as they are in that photo. They fill about a third of the frame, sharply lit, looking toward the
graphic on the right.

Right two-thirds: [THE ONE GRAPHIC, e.g. the two uploaded logos side by side joined by one bold
arrow / a cropped piece of the result screen / a before-and-after]. Nothing else competes with it.

Background: [BACKGROUND COLOUR OR GRADIENT from the profile], simple, so the person and the graphic
stand out. Accent in [ACCENT COLOUR, not blue/red/white/black] on [the arrow / a glow / the text
block].

Text: only these words, in a heavy bold sans-serif like [FONT], [TEXT COLOUR] with a thick dark
outline and a soft shadow: "[PAYOFF, 3 WORDS]".

Keep all text and the face out of the bottom-right corner and away from the edges. Two or three
colours in total. Photorealistic person, crisp logos, no clutter, no extra icons.
```

## Prompt template: concept B (graphic only)

```
A YouTube thumbnail, 1280x720, 16:9, minimal and high-contrast, readable at small phone size.

Centre: [THE ONE GRAPHIC, e.g. logo A on the left and logo B on the right, both large, joined by
one bold [ACCENT COLOUR] arrow flowing from A into B]. These are the only graphic elements.

Optional payoff cue: a small cropped glimpse of [THE RESULT, e.g. a chart going up] with a small
pointer to it.

Background: [BACKGROUND from the profile, e.g. off-white paper texture or a dark solid colour],
whichever makes the graphic stand out most.

Text: 3 words max, heavy bold sans-serif like [FONT], [TEXT COLOUR] with a dark outline:
"[PAYOFF, 3 WORDS]".

Keep text out of the bottom-right corner and away from the edges. Two or three colours. No extra
icons, no busy screenshots.
```

**No text on thumbnails?** If the profile says no text, drop the text line and let the graphic carry the payoff alone. The overlap test then applies to the image: it should show something the title doesn't say.

**Another image tool** (Midjourney, Ideogram, Canva, Firefly, Gemini)? Keep the same content, but follow that tool's habits: Midjourney wants a shorter, comma-separated prompt with `--ar 16:9` and can't keep a real face from a photo reliably, so suggest adding the photo afterwards in a design tool. Ideogram is good at text. In Canva, give layout instructions instead of a prompt.
